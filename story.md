# The Heron's Hour

Marta had learned that the secret to birding was not arriving early, but arriving *before* the world decided what it wanted to be. So at 4:40 in the morning, she stood at the edge of Fenwick Marsh with a thermos of coffee and her grandfather's battered binoculars, watching the dark water do nothing.

The marsh had been her grandfather's church. He'd taken her there every October until the year his knees finally refused the boardwalk, and then he'd sat in the car park with the radio on low, asking afterward for the full list: species, numbers, weather. He kept a ledger going back to 1962. Forty-one years of tallies in blue ballpoint, the handwriting getting shakier but never stopping.

He had died in March. The ledger was hers now, and this was her first visit without him.

At 5:15, the sky began its slow negotiation with the horizon — first a bruised grey, then a thin wash of rose in the still water. A moorhen complained somewhere in the reeds. Then another. The reeds began to stir, not with wind but with the small migrations of things waking.

Marta raised the binoculars and waited.

The grey heron came at 5:22, exactly as the ledger said it would. *5:20–5:40, heron, east bank, fishing*, her grandfather had written on this date in 1998, and again in 2004, and a dozen times since. The heron did not know about the ledger. The heron knew only the light and the tide and the small silver movements in the shallows, and it came because the marsh had always fed it. But Marta felt the fold of its wings land in her chest like a hand on the shoulder.

She counted. She noted. Dunnock. Reed bunting, two. A Cetti's warbler shouted from cover — heard, not seen, the way her grandfather liked to say the marsh kept some of its birds as rumor only.

By seven the light had gone honest and ordinary, the dog walkers arrived, and the magic thinned into daylight. Marta walked the boardwalk back with her list — nineteen species, modest by his standards — and thought about what she would tell him. Nothing, now. That was the shape of it. You collect all this counting and naming, and the one person you most want to recite it to is gone.

But then, near the car park, a bird dropped out of a birch tree and landed on the gravel six feet away: a robin, red-breasted, head cocked, entirely unafraid. It looked at her with the frank curiosity of a thing that has never been prey.

"Hello," she said out loud, and her voice cracked on the second syllable.

The robin stayed a long moment, then flicked away into the hedge.

On the drive home, Marta stopped at a stationery shop and bought a new ledger. The first line she wrote was the date, then: *5:22, heron, east bank, right on time.* Underneath, in smaller letters: *He would have loved this one.*
